# The Sunset Scene

> [!question] Open exploration
> This piece hasn't been written yet. What follows describes what it needs to be and what questions remain open.

## What This Needs to Be

A short narrative piece — 2-4 pages — showing a day in a community that lives in the arbor. Not utopia. Not perfection. Just the absence of the contraction at community scale.

This is the Roddenberry move (see [[Lenses#Gene Roddenberry — Showing the Future to Build It]]): show people living in a post-scarcity world so vividly that readers start wanting to build it. Not because they've been convinced — because they've tasted it in Steps 1-3 and now they can see what it looks like at scale.

## What It Must Do

- **Feel like recognition, not fantasy.** Readers who've experienced the opening (individually or in a Campfire) should read this and think "yes, that's what it felt like, but bigger." Without that prior experience, it risks reading as naive fiction.
- **Show problems without dread.** Things go wrong in this community. Someone trips, someone catches them, nobody keeps score. A decision needs making — it flows through consent. The problems are real; the contraction around them is absent.
- **Make AI feel natural.** AI weaves through daily life as structure, not surveillance. It's the arbor — facilitating, not controlling. Readers should barely notice it, the way you barely notice the trellis when the garden is blooming.
- **Be a scene, not a manifesto.** No character explains how the economics work. No one delivers a speech about governance. The structure shows in how people behave, not in what they say about it.

## Banks' Culture as Design Reference

Iain M. Banks wrote dozens of these scenes across his Culture novels — daily life on orbitals and ships where abundance is the baseline. His work is the most thorough thought experiment available for what this feels like from the inside. Key textures to draw from:

- **Choosing projects because they're interesting, not because they generate income.** Culture citizens design habitats, compose music, study obscure civilizations, play elaborate games, explore dangerous environments — because they want to. The absence of economic compulsion doesn't produce laziness. It produces fierce engagement with things that actually matter to you.
- **AI woven into the environment like weather.** You notice it when it's useful, forget it when it's not. The ship Mind manages life support, logistics, scheduling — and occasionally offers a dry observation that makes you laugh. It's not surveillance. It's infrastructure with a personality. (In the near-term Sunset Scene, before Minds, this is infrastructure without personality — but equally seamless.)
- **Disagreements that are vigorous but not contracted.** People argue passionately about aesthetics, ethics, priorities. But nobody's survival is at stake, so the arguments don't have teeth. You can be wrong without losing anything. This changes the texture of conflict entirely — it becomes play, not war.
- **The honest texture of restlessness.** Banks was too honest to pretend abundance eliminates the desire for challenge. Some Culture citizens get bored. Some seek danger deliberately — climbing without safety, joining Contact to explore dangerous civilizations, playing high-stakes games. Abundance doesn't eliminate the need for meaning; it frees it from desperation. **The Sunset Scene should show someone reaching for a challenge, not just resting in comfort.**

These aren't design constraints. They're what the scene should feel like when it's working.

## Open Questions

- What medium? Text, video, VR, all three? (Disney lens: experienceable spaces)
- What community? A neighborhood? A workplace? A mixed setting showing multiple contexts in one day?
- Whose perspective? One person's day? Multiple viewpoints? An outsider visiting for the first time?
- How much should it echo recognizable present-day life vs. feel distinctly different?
- The writer needs to have experienced Campfires before writing this well. Can it be sketched now and rewritten after that experience?
