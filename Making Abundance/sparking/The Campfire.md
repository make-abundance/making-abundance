# The Campfire

> [!info] This is a design
> The Campfire has been designed but not yet facilitated. The four-movement structure described here will evolve once real groups experience it.

## What It Is

A 90-minute facilitated experience for 8-12 people. SI-facilitated throughout. The purpose: let a group of people feel the arbor for the first time. Not understand it — feel it.

This is the Disneyland of the movement (Disney lens). A bounded space where the rules work differently. You walk in carrying the contraction. You walk out having felt what it's like to set it down with other people.

This is the Dagobah of the movement (Lucas lens). The place where "try harder" dissolves into "let go." Where the protagonist discovers the power was always there — blocked, not absent.

This is the fractal seed of the movement (Brown lens). What happens here at small scale IS the movement. If this doesn't work with 12 people, it won't work with 12 million. If it does, scaling is just more of it.

## The Four Movements

### Movement 1 — Feel the Contraction (20 minutes)

**Purpose:** Help participants recognize the contraction in their own bodies, right now, in this room.

**Flow:**
- The SI facilitator guides a brief noticing exercise. Not meditation — just observation.
- Prompt: "Think about a decision your team, family, or community is facing. Notice what happens in your body when you imagine the group choosing the option you don't want."
- Pause. Let people notice.
- "That tightness? That bracing? That's the contraction. That's the effort of scarcity. You've been doing that all day, every day, in every meeting, for years."
- Brief sharing: what did you notice? Where in your body? (Keep it short — 2-3 people, 1-2 sentences each.)

**Key principles:**
- No jargon. No theory. Just noticing.
- The SI doesn't lecture — it asks questions and holds space.
- Participants name their own experience. The facilitator doesn't name it for them.
- Broderick lens: meet people where they are. Some will feel a lot. Some won't. Both are fine.

### Movement 2 — The Decider, First Round (20 minutes)

**Purpose:** Make a real decision together using the Decider Protocol. Experience what happens when structure replaces hierarchy.

**The Decider Protocol:**
1. Someone proposes something specific. ("I propose we [specific action].")
2. Everyone votes simultaneously: thumbs up (support), thumbs down (paramount objection), thumbs sideways (will support but have concerns).
3. If all thumbs up or sideways: the proposal passes. Sideways voters can briefly share concerns for the group to consider.
4. If any thumbs down: the proposer asks "What would it take to get you to thumbs up?" The proposal iterates or the proposer withdraws.

**The arbor move:** When the group diverges — when there are thumbs down or strong sideways — the SI doesn't drive toward convergence. Instead:

> "It sounds like there are multiple directions here that people feel strongly about. What if we didn't have to pick just one? What would it take to explore two or three of these?"

This is the moment the contraction releases. The assumption that the group must converge — that opportunity is scarce, that my idea has to win or it dies — dissolves when the facilitator reveals that multiple paths are possible.

The groupthink scarcity — the worry that everyone will have to do the same thing, so I have to make sure MY thing wins — turns out to be an illusion. An artificial shade that was blocking multiple paths to light.

**Key principles:**
- The decision must be real. Not a simulation. Something that actually matters to this group.
- The SI helps people articulate proposals crisply (this is where SI-as-arbor starts becoming tangible — it supports expression, not control).
- When divergence happens, the SI treats it as a FEATURE, not a problem. "Great — we have three genuine directions. That's more information than forced agreement would give us."

### Movement 3 — Branch, Explore, Merge (30 minutes)

**Purpose:** Actually explore multiple options simultaneously. Experience the arbor in action. Learn branching — the operational pattern of abundance.

**The branching frame:**
The facilitator (SI or human) names what's about to happen: "We're going to branch. Each group is going to explore a direction that is *probably wrong*. That's the point. You don't need to be right — you need to find things that are useful, interesting, or challenging. Then we'll come back together and merge: choose what to keep."

This framing is critical. **"Probably wrong" is the release.** It dissolves the contraction around being right. When everyone is exploring something probably wrong, no one has to defend their direction. No one has to win. The identity-tied-to-opinion contraction drops away because the structure has made it safe to be wrong.

**Flow:**
- The group splits into 2-3 small groups, each pursuing a different option from the divergence in Movement 2.
- Each subgroup gets its own SI facilitator. Each SI helps its group thoroughly explore their direction — not superficially, not defensively, but with genuine curiosity.
- 20 minutes of real work. What would this look like? What problems does it solve? What does it miss? What's surprising?
- The SIs have no stake. They help each direction develop fully. A critic who isn't critical — supporting rigorous exploration without any judgment about which direction is "better."
- Come back together. Each subgroup surfaces what they found. Not a pitch — a report. "Here's what we discovered. Here's what was useful. Here's what was surprising."
- The group merges: what do we want to keep from each branch? The SI helps synthesize, but the group decides what to blend, what to carry forward, what to set aside.

**The arbor test in action:** The group now makes the NEXT decision with actual evidence from multiple paths, not hypothetical arguments about which path MIGHT work. This is profoundly different from normal decision-making, where options die the moment the group "picks one."

**Key principles:**
- The subgroups are exploring, not competing. There's no "winning team." Everyone is exploring something probably wrong.
- Each SI helps its direction develop fully, without bias. The SI can't find any direction unworthy — it has no stakes.
- When the group reconvenes, the merge often reveals that elements from multiple branches coexist — or that the exploration surfaced something none of the branches had considered alone.
- This is the experiential proof that divergence is wealth, not waste.
- Nothing is lost. Even branches that aren't merged remain available. The group's full exploration is preserved.

### Movement 4 — Name What Happened (20 minutes)

**Purpose:** Let the group name their own experience. This is where belief follows behavior (Broderick lens).

**Flow:**
- Open reflection. The SI asks gentle prompts:
  - "What felt different about that process?"
  - "Where did you notice the contraction release?"
  - "What was it like to not have to win?"
  - "What would it mean if you could make decisions this way at work? At home?"
- The group names their own experience. The SI captures themes but doesn't drive conclusions.
- No doctrine. No "and THAT is what abundance means." Just: what happened? What did you feel? What do you want to take with you?

**Key principles:**
- The SI is an arbor here too — creating structure for reflection without constraining what people say.
- Resist the urge to explain. Let the experience do the work.
- Some people will connect this to bigger themes. Some won't yet. Both are fine.
- End with a simple question: "What's one scarcity behavior you could set down this week?" Not as an assignment. As an invitation.

## The Role of SI

The SI in the Campfire is the most concrete demonstration of the arbor pattern. It:

- **Facilitates without controlling.** Keeps the process flowing, doesn't determine outcomes.
- **Supports expression.** Helps people articulate proposals, concerns, and reflections crisply.
- **Holds divergence.** When the group splits, the SI doesn't treat it as a problem to solve but as information to explore.
- **Synthesizes without flattening.** Helps the group see patterns across explorations without reducing three paths to one.
- **Creates safety for honest response.** People may be more willing to thumbs-down or express concerns when the SI normalizes divergence.

This is a radical reframe. Not AI-as-tool (do what I say). Not AI-as-threat (it's replacing me). SI-as-arbor: structure that multiplies the group's capacity to think, decide, and act together. (See [[AI as Arbor#Three Levels of Artificial Intelligence]] for why this is specifically an SI task — the lack of selfhood is the feature.)

**The tension to manage:** Many people currently associate AI with surveillance, control, and job displacement (all ML-driven harms). The Campfire needs to demonstrate a different relationship with intelligence — but it can't do that by arguing. It can only do it by being experienced. People who sit in an SI-facilitated Campfire and feel the contraction release will have a different felt relationship with artificial intelligence than people who've only encountered it as ML-driven threat.

## Design Principles

- **Real stakes.** The decision must matter. Simulations teach nothing about the contraction because the contraction only engages around real stakes.
- **No spectators.** Everyone participates. The contraction is felt in the body, not observed from the outside.
- **No doctrine.** The Campfire never explains the theory. It facilitates an experience and lets participants name what happened.
- **Repeatable.** The Campfire is designed to be run again and again, with different groups, different decisions. Each one is a seed of the fractal (Brown lens).
- **Trainable.** Campfire facilitation must be learnable. The SI carries much of the process, which means human facilitators need to learn how to partner with the SI, not how to memorize a script.

## Open Questions

- What SI model/interface is right for this? Voice? Text on screen? A custom interface?
- How do you train Campfire facilitators? What's the "train the campfire" experience?
- What's the minimum viable Campfire? Can it be done in 60 minutes? 45? At what point does compression lose the essential experience?
- Virtual or in-person? Both have tradeoffs. In-person feels more real. Virtual is more accessible. Can the arbor pattern work in both?
- How do you select the first Campfire participants? Should they be strangers, coworkers, friends? Mixed?
- What decisions work best for the first Campfire? Team decisions? Community decisions? Something the group brings, or something provided?
- How does the "come back and see how multiple options worked out" pattern play out over longer timescales? Is there a multi-session Campfire design?
