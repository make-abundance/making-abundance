# The Arbor

## The Design Pattern

An arbor is **structure that reveals rather than constrains**.

A garden arbor is a simple framework — wood or wire — that multiplies the growing space available to plants. Without it, plants compete for a single shaft of light, growing over and around each other, strangling, shading, fighting for the one path upward. With it, there are many paths to light and air. Each plant finds its own direction. They support each other on the structure. Growth is unstructured, diverse, and abundant — *because* the structure is there.

This is not a metaphor. It is a design pattern that appears at every scale:

| Scale | Without Arbor | With Arbor |
|-------|--------------|------------|
| Individual awareness | Effortful seeking, contraction | Recognition of what's already here (Loch Kelly) |
| Group decisions | One option wins, others die | Multiple options explored simultaneously (Decider + AI) |
| Organizations | Hierarchy directs growth | Sociocratic circles: structure that enables self-organization |
| Manufacturing | Inspection catches defects | System conditions produce quality naturally (Deming) |
| Engineering | Heavy structures, brute force | Minimal structure, maximum enclosed space (Fuller) |
| Economics | Artificial scarcity, competition for access | Abundance engine: structure that distributes access |
| Social movements | Central plan, compliance | Fractal pattern: small-scale practice becomes large-scale (Brown) |

## Three Ways to Organize — Only One Is an Arbor

### Hierarchy (structure that constrains)
Growth is directed. The structure determines where things go. Efficient for known paths. Kills exploration. Creates bottlenecks, single points of failure, and power concentration. When the directing intelligence is wrong, the whole system fails.

### Anarchy (no structure)
No structure at all. Maximum freedom in theory. In practice, plants strangle each other. Informal hierarchies emerge (the tallest plant shades the rest). The strong dominate by default. Without structure, the contraction intensifies — everyone must compete because nothing protects them.

### Arbor (structure that reveals)
Simple structure. Not directing growth — enabling it. Multiplying the paths available. Creating space where diversity is natural because there's room for it. Plants don't have to conform or fight or chase a single shaft of light. They find themselves with sun in all directions, free to grow in any way and to support and be supported by others.

The arbor is the third thing. Not hierarchy, not anarchy. Structure in service of opening.

## The Arbor at Each Scale

### In Awareness (Loch Kelly)
The pointing-out instruction is an arbor. It's a minimal structure — a question, a direction of attention — that reveals the awareness already present. It doesn't create awareness. It creates the conditions for you to notice what was always there.

### In Decisions (The Decider Protocol)
The Decider Protocol is an arbor for group decisions. Its structure:
1. Someone proposes
2. Everyone votes simultaneously (thumbs up/down/sideways)
3. If divergence: "What would it take?" or explore multiple options

The key arbor move: **when the group diverges, you don't force convergence.** You ask: "What if both paths could be tried? What would that take?" This reveals that the scarcity of options was artificial. The assumption that the group must all do the same thing — that opportunity is scarce and I must make sure my idea wins — dissolves when the structure makes room for multiple explorations.

### In AI Facilitation
AI-as-arbor: the AI facilitates without controlling. It keeps things flowing, helps when divergence creates confusion, supports the group in exploring multiple options simultaneously, and helps them come back together to compare results. It doesn't decide. It creates structure that enables the group's own intelligence.

This is a radical reframe from AI-as-threat. The AI is not replacing human judgment. It's an arbor — structure that multiplies the group's growing space.

### In Organizations (Sociocratic Governance)
Nested circles. Each circle has autonomy within its domain. Decisions by consent (no one has a paramount objection), not consensus (everyone agrees) or hierarchy (one person decides). Double-linking connects circles without creating hierarchy.

This is the arbor at organizational scale. The structure doesn't direct — it connects and enables.

### In Economics (The Abundance Engine)
The core engine (solar + robotics + AI + materials) is an arbor for material goods. It creates structure (supply chains, automation, distribution) that multiplies access. As production costs approach zero, the remaining constraint is organizational — who owns the structure and how decisions are made about it.

### In Movements (Emergent Strategy)
Adrienne Maree Brown: what you practice at the small scale sets the patterns for the whole system. The campfire IS the movement. The arbor pattern at 12 people IS the arbor pattern at 12 million. Scale is fractal, not hierarchical.

## The Arbor Test

The keystone commitment (Paul O'Neill pattern):

> **"Does this decision increase the number of good paths available, or decrease it?"**

Every decision, every action, every policy gets filtered through this question. Like O'Neill's safety metric at Alcoa, this is simple enough for anyone to apply, anywhere, immediately. It forces all the right behaviors without prescribing specific outcomes:

- If a governance decision closes options → it fails the arbor test
- If a business strategy creates dependency → it fails the arbor test
- If a communication approach requires agreement → it fails the arbor test
- If a facilitation technique drives toward convergence when divergence is natural → it fails the arbor test

The test doesn't tell you what to do. It tells you what not to do: **don't build shade.**

## The Relationship Between Arbor and Contraction

The contraction is what happens when there's no arbor — or when the arbor has been replaced by artificial shade (hierarchy, artificial scarcity, forced convergence).

Under shade, plants contract. They compete. They strain toward the one shaft of light.

The arbor removes the shade. In the presence of the arbor, the contraction releases naturally. Not because you fought it — because the conditions changed. Sun in all directions. Room to grow in any way. Support available from the structure and from other plants.

This is why the sequence works: name the contraction (so people can feel it), then show them the arbor (so they can feel it release). You don't have to convince anyone to let go. You just have to change the conditions.

## Open Questions

- What arbor structures already exist that people are in but don't recognize as arbors? (Open source communities? Certain kinds of neighborhoods? Farmer's markets?)
- How do you build an arbor for something that IS genuinely scarce (housing, healthcare in the current system)?
- What's the minimum viable arbor? The smallest structure that demonstrates the pattern?
- Can the arbor test be gamed? What would gaming it look like, and how would you defend against it?
