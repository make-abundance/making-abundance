# The Sequence

> [!info] This is a design
> The sequence has been mapped out but not yet tested. The steps described here are the recommended path — the actual experience of running them will reshape the design.

## Why Sequence Matters

The sequence is not arbitrary. Each step creates a precondition for the next. You can't feel the arbor before you've named the contraction. You can't write the Sunset Scene before you've experienced the campfire. You can't absorb the engine room before you've felt the opening.

This follows from multiple lenses:
- **Loch Kelly:** You can't explain awareness. You can only point to it. The pointing has to come before the understanding.
- **Broderick:** Behavior creates belief, not the other way around. Experience before explanation.
- **Lucas:** The audience feels the ache before they understand the world. Twin suns before the briefing room.
- **Meadows:** Highest leverage first. The paradigm shift (contraction → opening) must precede the structural changes (governance, economics).
- **Brown:** Get the small pattern right. The large pattern is just more of it.

## The Six Steps

### Step 1: Name the Contraction

**Format:** Short written piece. Blog post length. 800-1200 words.

**Purpose:** Walk the reader through recognizing the contraction in their own body. Not arguing that scarcity is artificial — letting them *feel* the effort of maintaining it. End with: "You've been doing this all day. You can set it down."

**Why it comes first:** Everything else depends on people recognizing the contraction. If they don't feel it, the rest is just ideology. If they do feel it, the rest is just support for what they've already started.

**What must be true before this step:** Nothing. This is the entry point. It requires no prior knowledge, no buy-in, no community. One person, reading alone, noticing their own jaw tightening.

**Success looks like:** The reader sends it to someone with the message: "This is the thing I couldn't explain."

### Step 2: The Pointing-Out Piece

**Format:** Short experiential piece. 600-1000 words. Loch Kelly-style.

**Purpose:** Guide the reader through one moment of opening. Not just naming the contraction but actually releasing it, briefly, on the page. Let them feel the relief.

**Why it comes second:** Step 1 names the problem. Step 2 offers the taste of the alternative. Without step 1, the opening has no contrast — you don't notice dry air unless you've been soaking wet. Without step 2, step 1 is just a diagnosis with no treatment.

**What must be true before this step:** The reader has recognized the contraction. They know the feeling. Now they're ready to set it down for 30 seconds.

**Success looks like:** The reader sits back from the screen, takes a breath, and feels something shift. Even briefly. Even slightly. That's enough.

### Step 3: The Campfire

**Format:** 90-minute facilitated group experience. 8-12 people. SI-facilitated. See [[The Campfire]] for full design.

**Purpose:** Experience the arbor in a group. Feel what happens when a room full of people set down the contraction simultaneously. Make a real decision using the Decider Protocol. Discover that multiple paths can coexist. Experience SI as arbor — structure that enables, not controls.

**Why it comes third:** Steps 1 and 2 are individual. Step 3 is the first group experience. The reader has felt the contraction and the opening alone — now they discover it's possible with others. This is where "I can set it down" becomes "we can set it down."

**What must be true before this step:** Participants have at least read step 1 (ideally both 1 and 2). They arrive already able to notice the contraction. This means the facilitator doesn't have to convince anyone — just guide the experience.

**Success looks like:** Someone in the group says something like "I didn't realize how hard I was working to make my idea win." Or: "I can't believe we just explored three options and all of them worked." The group leaves having felt the arbor, not just understood it.

### Step 4: The Sunset Scene

**Format:** Short story / narrative piece. 2-4 pages.

**Purpose:** Show a day in a community that lives in the arbor. Not utopia — but the absence of the contraction at community scale. Problems without dread. Someone trips, someone catches them, nobody keeps score. Decisions flow through consent. SI weaves through daily life as structure, not surveillance. Everything is simple, curious, open-hearted.

**Why it comes fourth:** Writing this well requires having experienced the campfire. And reading it well requires having experienced the opening. Without personal experience of the arbor, this reads as fantasy. With it, it reads as recognition — "yes, that's what it felt like, but bigger."

**What must be true before this step:** The writer has run campfires and seen the opening happen in groups. The reader has felt the opening (individually or in a campfire) and can project it forward.

**Success looks like:** The Roddenberry effect. People who read it start wanting to build it. Not because they've been convinced — because they've tasted it (steps 1-3) and now they've seen what it looks like at scale. The desire to build becomes natural, not recruited.

### Step 5: The Arbor Test — The Keystone Commitment

**Format:** Short declaration. Fits on a card. Plus a longer companion piece explaining the pattern.

**Purpose:** Give people one question to carry into every decision: "Does this open growing space or close it?" This is the O'Neill move — one metric that forces all the right behaviors. This is what people take back into their teams, families, and communities. The daily practice that sustains the opening.

**Why it comes fifth:** The test only makes sense after you've felt the contraction (so you know what "closing" feels like) and the opening (so you know what "opening" feels like). Without the felt experience, it's just a slogan.

**What must be true before this step:** Felt experience of both contraction and opening. Ideally, experience in a campfire where the test was implicitly applied (the Decider opens space; forced convergence closes it).

**Success looks like:** People start using it. In meetings: "Wait — does this close options or open them?" In hiring: "Does this person help us grow in more directions?" In relationships: "Am I building shade or building arbor right now?" It becomes a habit. A reflex.

### Step 6: The Engine Room

**Format:** The existing documents in `core-idea/`, sequenced and refined.

**Purpose:** For people who've felt the opening and want to understand the structure. How the economics work. How steward ownership protects against capture. How sociocratic governance scales. How the abundance engine produces material abundance. How defenses against scarcity behavior are built into the structure.

**Why it comes last:** Nobody tours the engine room before deciding to board. But everyone who's decided to board wants to see the engine room. These documents become the answer to "How does this actually work?" — a question people only ask after they've felt the pull.

**What must be true before this step:** The reader has experienced the opening (individually and/or in a group), has seen the vision (Sunset Scene), and has the daily practice (Arbor Test). Now curiosity about the mechanism is natural.

**Success looks like:** The reader moves from "this sounds nice" to "this is structurally sound." The paranoid governance defenses (which might feel excessive without context) now feel reassuring — "they've thought about how this could go wrong." The economic case (which might feel like a lecture without the feeling) now feels like confirmation — "so THAT'S why it works."

## The Sequence as Arbor

Notice: the sequence itself is an arbor. It doesn't force one path. People might:
- Read Step 1 and immediately run a Campfire (skipping Step 2)
- Experience a Campfire first and go back to read Steps 1 and 2 afterward
- Start with the Sunset Scene because someone shared it, then work backward
- Jump to the Engine Room because they're structurally minded, then go back for the feeling

The sequence is the recommended path — the one that builds each precondition most naturally. But the arbor allows many paths to light. Each entry point can lead to any other. The wikilinks between documents are the branches of the arbor.

## Open Questions

- What's the timeline? Is Step 1 a week of writing? A day? How fast can we iterate?
- Who runs the first campfires? How do facilitators get trained? Is there a "train the campfire" design?
- Does the Sunset Scene exist as text? As video? As VR? As all three? (Disney lens: experienceable spaces)
- Should there be a Step 0 — something even before naming the contraction? A question? An image? A single sentence that someone encounters and can't shake?
- How do we know the sequence is working? What does measurement look like without creating a contraction (metrics pressure)?
